# Proposal

How to shape an enterprise proposal on the method in `method.md`.
Section numbers in brackets point there.

## 1. Inputs

Fill these from the deal sheet before writing a heading. Mark a gap
`[OPEN: question]` and ask the user once, in one batch.

1. Game: enterprise confirmed [1].
2. Readers: the champion, the executive who signs, and any risk,
   technology, legal or procurement reviewer. What each one needs from
   the document.
3. The buyer's own numbers and words, each with its source (call, email,
   their report).
4. The gap in two sentences [3].
5. First stage: contained scope, who gets access, deliverables,
   measures, exit gate, timeline [4], [7].
6. Later stages: years two and three [4].
7. Price: one anchor and the structure options [4], [6].
8. What is not included, and what we cannot do today [8].
9. Added value at no charge: cheap to us, worth a lot to them [6].
10. Decision: who approves, by what date, what happens in week one.

## 2. Structure

Numbered headings. Adjust to the deal and drop a section that has
nothing to say.

1. Summary. One page the champion can forward on its own: where they
   are, the opportunity, the first stage, the investment, the decision
   needed and by when.
2. Current State. Their numbers and their words, sourced.
3. Opportunity. What they can do after the first stage that they cannot
   do now, how it is measured, and what it means for their position in
   their market.
4. First Stage. Scope, who gets access, deliverables, measures, exit
   gate, timeline.
5. Not Included. What the first stage does not do, and current limits.
6. Later Stages. Year two and year three direction.
7. Investment. The anchored price for the contained scope, the
   structure options, the term, and what is included at no charge.
8. Risk and Controls. When the buyer is regulated or asked for it.
9. Decision and Next Steps. Who approves, by when, week one.

## 3. Rules

1. Lead with the opportunity. The problem is evidence under it [3].
2. One anchored price for a contained scope. Not a menu of plans, and
   no self-serve or SMB price list in the document [1], [4].
3. Options are structures around the anchor: scope, a year-two step-up,
   term. Never a discount off it [6].
4. Say what we cannot do. Overselling costs trust and causes churn [8].
5. No competitor comparison. It invites the one-of-three frame [5].
6. Assume an AI reads it for the buyer. Every number has a source, and
   every step-up in price is tied to added value [4].
7. Keep internal words out of the document: alpha, vision casting, go
   to bat, Mario, gap selling. Write the concrete outcome instead.
8. The first stage asks for as little data and change as possible, and
   can be reversed [7].

## 4. Writing

Jen's motto is to be direct and cut the fluff: "give me the bullet not
the paragraph."

- Headings are labels, not arguments. "4. First Stage", not "Why we
  start small".
- Short declarative sentences. Claim, evidence and source together.
- Lists over paragraphs. Exact numbers.
- No hype, no metaphors, no closing summary that repeats the document.
- Match the buyer's spelling and date conventions.
- Keep a sentence only if deleting it would change a decision or leave
  a question unanswered.

## 5. Champion Kit

Half a page for the champion's own use, separate from the proposal.

1. The case for their executive in one paragraph, in the champion's
   voice and the executive's terms.
2. What the champion got out of us: the structure, the no-charge items,
   the step-up timing. This is their win to carry upstairs [6].
3. The three questions the executive, risk or procurement will ask, with
   answers.
4. The question we ask the champion once the proposal lands [9]:
   "Honestly, can we get this approved by [date]? What would stop it?"

## 6. Reviewing a Draft

Read the whole draft. List at most seven changes, most important first.
Each gives the location (section and line), the rule in section 3 or 4
it breaks, and the rewrite. Skip changes that do not move the decision.
