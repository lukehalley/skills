# Method

Jen Abel's enterprise sales method, from Lenny's Podcast, "Sell the
alpha, not the feature": The enterprise sales playbook for $1M to $10M
ARR (9 November 2025). Jen Abel is co-founder of Jellyfish and GM of
Enterprise at State Affairs.

Episode: https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/sell-the-alpha-not-the-feature-the-enterprise/id1627920305?i=1000735957465

This file paraphrases the episode for practical use. Short quotes are
Jen's words as spoken, from the episode captions. Lines credited to
Lenny are the host's. Unofficial: not affiliated with Jen Abel,
Jellyfish or Lenny's Podcast. Listen to the episode for full context.

Her numbers are US enterprise figures in US dollars from her own deals.
They are not a market rate and not anyone's price list.

## 0. Cheat Sheet

1. Pick the game: enterprise or SMB. Mid-market is one or the other.
2. Tier one logos are the early adopters. Go to them early.
3. Sell the gap (where they could be), not the problem (what is broken).
4. Land at an enterprise price. Her range: US$75k to US$150k for a
   first contract.
5. A buyer who nickel-and-dimes is not bought in. Treat it as a
   qualification signal, not a pricing problem.
6. The land price is the reference point for every expansion.
7. Never become one of three in a comparison. Differentiate.
8. Craft the deal: co-author the price, give what is cheap to you and
   valuable to them.
9. Enterprises know how to buy services. Enter through a service, then
   move them to the product.
10. Design partners pay something, get a framed concession, and hear
    what you cannot do.
11. Every first call ends in a yes or a no. Ask the question you are
    afraid to ask.
12. Stuck in procurement usually means you sold too junior. Get the
    executive.
13. Objection: agree, then reframe to the opportunity. Never argue.
14. Outbound by hand. Three sentences, something counterintuitive,
    people not in the databases.
15. Relationships close enterprise deals. Pick up the phone.
16. Don't be better, be different.

## 1. Pick the Game

- Small business is marketing-led. Enterprise is sales-led. Selling to
  a 100-person company and a 1,000-person company are different games
  with no hybrid.
- Mid-market is either the top of SMB or the bottom of enterprise, and
  it is usually the bottom of enterprise. Decide which, then hire, price
  and sell for that game. Mixing them loses: "if you bleed those two
  games, you're going to lose."
- Deal size sets the motion. A US$5k deal needs a marketing-led engine.
  A US$100k deal means outbound from day one.
- An enterprise that arrives through self-serve still gets an
  enterprise deal. A small first price becomes the documented reference,
  and the buyer will ask to defend the jump: "I just paid $9,000 last
  year and now you want to charge me $90,000."
- A large SMB business starts at zero in enterprise: a new value
  proposition, deal structure, target market and risk tolerance.
- Which game to pick: the one the founder understands best. A marketer
  with an audience edge suits SMB. Someone who understands how large
  corporations buy, and wants US$100k+ deals, suits enterprise.

Apply:

- Classify the deal before anything else. Enterprise signals: an
  executive signs, a risk, security, legal or vendor review, a
  procurement step, several stakeholders, a staged rollout.
- An enterprise buyer asking for the self-serve plan is the trap above.
  Structure it as an enterprise deal even when the first scope is small.

## 2. Tier One Logos First

- The category leader is the early adopter, because staying number one
  is the hardest part. A small edge is what gets someone there
  promoted: "if you can give me just a slight bit of alpha".
- Her examples of tier one: Walmart, McDonald's, Nvidia, Tesla, Exxon
  Mobil, UnitedHealthcare.
- Their voice grows the deal, from US$100k to US$1M "in a very short
  period of time."
- Numbers two to four copy the leader, so one tier one logo is a
  reference for the whole category. Tier one firms also have the most
  strategic executives and attract the best people.
- This runs against the common advice to learn down-market first. Her
  reason: in a fast market, getting in before a competitor matters more
  than a short sales cycle.
- Get the founder involved as early as possible. Buyers like talking to
  a founder.

Apply:

- Ask who leads the buyer's category. If this buyer is the leader, treat
  the deal as a reference asset.
- If the buyer is not the leader, name the leader and what winning this
  deal does for reaching them.

## 3. Sell the Gap, Not the Problem

- "You need to sell to a gap. Don't sell to a problem."
- Problem selling is specific and technical, and it is what every
  salesperson does: find the pain and anchor to it.
- Gap selling, which she calls vision casting, shows a leader where they
  could be: "they are here. Here's where we can take you."
- Kathy Sierra's image: don't sell the mushroom, sell Mario after he
  eats it. The buyer becomes the superhero.
- The opportunity is usually an edge others do not have, which she
  calls alpha: information, data or a way of working nobody else can
  offer. Her example is high-frequency trading, sold as a one-second
  head start rather than as fibre.
- Her Cursor example moves from "your engineers will be more
  productive" (problem) to talent (gap): "you'll be able to actually
  hire the 10x engineers".
- Nobody goes to bat inside their company for fixing a small problem.

Apply:

- Write the gap in two sentences. "You are here: [their state, in their
  numbers]." "Here is where we take you: [what they can do that peers
  cannot]."
- Test it: does it make the buyer look good to their boss and their
  market? Does it give them an edge over the number two? If it only
  removes a pain, it is still a problem pitch.
- Keep the problem as evidence under the gap. Their numbers prove the
  gap is real.
- "Alpha", "vision casting", "Mario" are internal words. In anything the
  buyer reads, write the concrete outcome.

## 4. Land at an Enterprise Price

- Founders discount by reflex, "till the cows come home", believing it
  closes deals.
- A buyer who nickel-and-dimes is "not fully bought in". The best
  clients do not do it. Read it as a qualification signal.
- Small deals mislead: a false sense of product-market fit, bad-fit
  customers who still need serving, and weak expansion (US$10k to
  US$15k is +50%; US$100k to US$500k comes naturally when they want
  more). A good enterprise rep will not work for commission on US$10k
  deals.
- A venture-backed company cannot sell US$10k deals into enterprise.
- Enterprises are used to a first contract "somewhere between 75K and
  150K".
- Start contained. State the price, who gets access, the value, and
  where it goes in years two and three: "Don't say 150k and sell the
  farm."
- An executive signing is worth two more months, because it proves they
  are bought in.
- A small land works only with a fast ramp, such as US$10k to US$50k to
  US$100k within a few months. A slow one fails: "$10,000 a year then
  going to 12 then going to 15." "The math will break."
- Small deals can still take months in procurement. She has seen a
  US$10k deal take nine months.
- The land price is the reference point for expansion, and buyers now
  read contracts with AI. A 10x price jump is hard to defend: "They're
  gonna want to see 15x value."
- The value must feel "way more than the cost."
- A service can be prorated monthly (her example: US$10k a month) so
  the total lands in the enterprise range.

Apply:

- One anchored number for a contained scope: who gets access, what
  value, where it goes in years two and three.
- Show the value arithmetic from the buyer's own numbers. The value must
  read as far larger than the price.
- A small first price needs a written ramp with dates and amounts, or
  it becomes the ceiling.
- A discount request is a qualification question first: is the
  executive bought in? Then trade, never give (section 6).

## 5. Never One of Three

- Being one of three vendors "they're testing out" means "you've
  already sort of lost." The answer is differentiation.
- The product is not only what goes into their hands. It is also the
  pricing, the opportunity and the framing, and not letting them
  compare you to something else.
- The pitch is what they can do tomorrow because of how you serve them
  today.
- Use your own language. Copying a trend's name (her example: renaming
  forward deployed engineers) makes you look like everyone else.
- "don't be better, be different."

Apply:

- In a bake-off: move the frame to the gap only you address, and ask to
  be judged on that measure. If the buyer will only compare features
  and price, qualify hard (section 9).
- Never put a competitor comparison table in a proposal.

## 6. Deal Crafting

- From US$1M to US$10M ARR, enterprise sales is "more of an art", and
  "it's all about deal crafting".
- Give things "that don't really cost much to you, but are super
  expensive for them". Her examples: a custom build for them, contained
  to a year, at no additional cost; a speaking slot at your event.
- Co-author the price. Her approach: name the engagement (her example:
  US$150k), offer to make it a bit bigger if they need more, or smaller
  in year one with a year-two step-up, and ask how to get it done "so
  when you go to bat it's a win".
- If they want a lower price, give it in exchange for a longer term.
- Consistent deal structures matter for deals under US$10k. Deals of
  US$100k and up usually look different every time.
- The buyer goes to bat for a new tool rarely, "once every two years
  maybe once every three years", and their reputation rides on it. They
  need to say what they got out of you.
- In her experience co-authoring rarely ends in being discounted: by
  then there is a relationship.

Apply. Structure options around one anchor, never discounts off it:

- full scope in year one;
- smaller year one with the year-two step-up written in;
- lower price for a longer term;
- added value at no charge: a contained custom integration, a named
  engineer, early roadmap access, a case study or speaking slot.

## 7. Services First

- Services are the budget line enterprises buy most and know how to
  buy: "the number one thing they buy services."
- When a buyer has never bought technology for the problem, sell the
  outcome as a service powered by your technology. Once in, move them to
  the product: "leverage the tool that's been powering this the whole
  time".
- At first they need not know software is doing the work.
- Start with low risk. "we won't even touch your data" is an easy way
  in. Begin with a low-risk subset of data, build trust, then connect
  the systems.
- Consultancies sit in their clients' offices. Forward deployed
  engineers follow the same pattern.
- Channel partners will not sell for you. You are one of a hundred on
  their list, and consultants are "not vision casters".

Apply:

- First stage needs no production data or a low-risk subset. Widen
  access stage by stage against measures.
- A service is quoted monthly and adds up to an enterprise land.
- Write the path from service to product into the proposal.

## 8. Design Partners

- Design partners are the hardest logos to convert to full rollout.
  They are a guide, not pipeline.
- Good fits are technology companies in the Fortune 1000, used to
  experimenting. A design partnership gives a large company a startup
  feel.
- The person matters most: keen to give feedback, bought into where you
  are going, and happy to "use a tool that's janky".
- Terms: they put skin in the game. You set the frame for future
  pricing: where it is going, where it is today, and their concession,
  for example a "30% concession in perpetuity" for being there on day
  one.
- Do not oversell. Say where you are today, what they get in the next
  six months, and what you cannot do: "Here's what we cannot do, which
  is just as important. It builds trust." Overselling causes churn and
  cancelled contracts.
- Most feedback reflects the old way of working. In one founder's
  partnerships it was about 80% noise and 20% gold. The founder decides:
  "I hear you, but here's why we're not going to do that."
- Close the terms with an alignment check: if we deliver what we say in
  the next 6 to 12 months, are we aligned?
- Converting a design partner to full rollout is a big win for the
  team and investors.

## 9. Qualify to a Yes or a No

- "I am a qualification crazy person."
- The first call ends in a yes or a no. A polite maybe is a no.
- Say it out loud: "I'm sort of getting the vibe that this might not be
  a good fit". When they agree, offer to stay in touch. That saves the
  relationship and your time.
- "Ask the questions you're afraid to".
- Later in a deal, not on day one: "Honestly, do we think we're going to
  get this the deal done this year?"
- Lenny describes a question she asks founders: would people use the
  product if you gave it away free? Founders say yes. Customers often
  say no.
- A procurement stall usually means the seller is not talking to a
  senior enough person: "that could just be a qualification error".
  When an executive rings their buying group, things move.

## 10. Objections

- Lenny, quoting her colleague: when you hit resistance, never argue,
  reframe.
- Her line for "we already have a tool": agree ("You're right. You have
  a tool for that."), then move to the opportunity: "We're taking you
  much further upstream with value."

Apply: agree with the true part, then move to the gap the existing tool
does not reach.

## 11. Relationship

- Her enterprise deals close by text message, not email. She answers
  enterprise clients immediately, and that builds trust.
- Buyers who trust you will "turn over rocks for you". Her example: she
  told a Fortune 10 buyer it mattered to her to close that year and
  asked whether it was possible. The buyer called it a tall order and
  agreed to try.
- Structure the deal so it is clear you went to bat for them.
- She calls bringing a visible AI recorder into a call "cringey".

## 12. Outbound

- She uses no prospecting tool: "I believe in the manual."
- AI prospecting tools all draw on the same databases, so the people in
  them are flooded. Write to someone "not in a database" and take the
  "back door".
- Every note is different. She reads visual cues: the photo, time in
  the role, time at the company.
- No AI-style personalised first line. Customise the framing and the
  subject line instead.
- Register follows the person: "QQ" for someone younger and casual; "a
  little bit of a tighter note, not all lowercase" for someone senior.
- "keep it to three sentences". "say something counterintuitive". The
  reader should feel they will learn something in a 15-minute call.
  "don't give away the farm."
- What she means to avoid: the generic "I came across your LinkedIn"
  line and a promise to grow their business by 15%.
- Timing counts. She wrote a one-sentence note to a busy chief legal
  officer at a hedge fund on a Saturday, and he replied.
- The effort is worth it: a US$100k deal can become US$1M over three to
  five years.
- Targets come from paying attention to the market, not from a list:
  who is in the news, who is experimenting, and the "next adjacent ring"
  of buyers.
- Do not be afraid to send 15 notes to people you can deliver serious
  value to.

## 13. First Enterprise Hire

- When: about US$1M ARR, with 7 to 10 customers and a pattern someone
  else can learn.
- From US$1M to US$10M the founder can no longer be in every deal. You
  need people who can "cosplay a founder": sell the vision, run through
  walls, craft each deal differently.
- Good profiles: a former founder; or someone with no sales background
  and deep product or engineering experience.
- Poor profiles: an SMB rep moved to enterprise; a VP of Sales from a
  big company, where "the brand was doing all of the work"; a junior
  rep with no corporate experience selling to executives.
- Test: would you want to buy from this person? Do they mirror the
  market they sell to? Executives want to talk to senior people. "the
  market doesn't want to be sold to, they want to buy."
- Pay: 50% base, 50% variable. Commission in technology is about 8% to
  12% of the deal, around 10%.
- Process: the founder joins the first five calls. About one in two
  enterprise hires fails, so hire two at once (advice she credits to
  Jason Lemkin).
